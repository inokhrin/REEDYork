Source description

Settlement: York
Repository: borthwick-institute
Shelfmark: E 20: 1327-1547

Three books containing copies of important civic and guild documents survive from this period. The most important and, unfortunately, the most badly damaged is the A/Y Memorandum Book. A/Y consists of three separate manuscripts bound together: the maior registrum, the novum registrum, and an incomplete paper index to the first two manuscripts and other registers, paper books, and books of acts. Although some documents enrolled in A/Y are from earlier in the fourteenth century, the compiling of the book in its original form was begun in January 1377.“ A/Y was a working book used and corrected by generations of civic officials. The entries are various in nature. Most are craft ordinances, often cancelled and updated. Some are civic ordinances recording regulations governing activities controlled by the city. These give us our earliest record of the Corpus Christi Play and the civic procession with the shrine of Corpus Christi. Occasionally, an entry resembles the later House Book material in recording the decisions of a particular council meeting. One important entry concerning the Corpus Christi celebration, the record of the events surrounding a sermon preached by Friar William Melton in 1426, was written into the record as a set piece of discursive prose. The earliest surviving reference to Corpus Christi pageants occurs in the first gathering of the book, recording the receipt of rent for storing pageants during the year 1376 in a form later used only in Bridgemasters’ account rolls. Entries on any one folio may come from widely separated years and may be written in different hands and different languages. We have transcribed all references to the Corpus Christi celebrations, even those that have been cancelled, since cancellation may have been made as long as a century after the original document was enrolled.

Technical description: Latin, Anglo-Norman, English; parchment with paper appendix; i+383+36 (paper); dimensions extremely variable: parchment approximately 285mm x 205mm (ruled space appoximately 238 mm x 145 mm), ff 333-40, approximately 270 mm x 175mm (203mm x 133mm), number of lines variable; (1) the maior registrum gatherings A-Z, mainly 4 and 6s, no signatures apparent, (2) the novum registrum gatherings a-q, 2s and 6s, signatures a-k, with gaps due to wear and trimming, (3) index, 10 gatherings of 2, except gathering 8, a singlet, and ff 35 and 36, detached leaves. Many inserted leaves, especially in the first 7 gatherings of (1) and gathering c of (2); frequent correction by later hands, cancellations, marginal notations in both contemporary and later hands; modern foliation at the foot of the leaf in pencil, treating (1) and (2) together, (3) separately, as do the 2 medieval folaitons, at the head of the leaf in ink. The medieval numberings show the original division of the manuscript: the first, 1-246, is overlapped by the second, 200 (beginning on the original 190) - 370, many cross references between sections, no medieval foliation in (3); little ornamentation in script; bound in white paper-covered boards resembling leather, red label on spine with title CITY OF YORK / - / REGISTER OF / VARIOUS MEMORANDA / RELATING TO CITY / - / 1327-1547. Beneath the label is A/Y, then 20. The manuscript has been heavily damaged by flooding, writing legible only under ultra-violet light on many leaves, in some entries (see the 1415 'Ordo' gathering) ink completely washed away, decipherable only in strong sunlight when outline of letters in clean parchment contrasts with surrounding darkened surface.

The Proclamation of the Corpus Christi Play, to be made at the Corpus Christi Watch:
Hear ye, etc. We command, on the king's behalf, and the mayor's, and the sheriffs of this city, that no man go armed in this city with swords nor with Carlisle axes, nor any other defences, in disturbance of the king's peace and the play, or hindering of the procession of Corpus Christi; and that they leave their armour in their lodging, except knights and squires of worship that ought to have swords borne after them, on pain of forfeiture of their weapon and imprisonment of their bodies; and those men that bring forth pageants, (we command) that they play at the places that are assigned thereto, and nowhere else, on the pain of forfeiture (of fees) to be raised (according to) what is ordained (therefore), that is to say, 40 shillings. And that men of crafts, and all other men that find torches, that they come forth in array and in the manner as it […] and according to custom before this time, none bearing weapons (saving kee)pers of the pageants, and officers that are keepers (of the peace,) on pain of forfeiture of their franchise, and their bodies to prison. And that all types of craftsmen that bring forth their pageants (do so) in order and course, by good players, well arrayed and openly speaking, upon pain of losing 100 shillings, to be paid to the chamber, without any pardon. And that every player that shall play be ready in his pageant at a convenient time, that is to say, at the midhour between four and five of the clock in the morning (4:30 AM), and then all other pageants fast following, each one after the other as their course is, without tarrying, on the pain of making (payment) to the chamber, 6 shillings and 8 pence. This 'ordo gathering' has been published in part by Drake in Eboracum, Smith in York Plays and Davies in Extracts. More recently, Martin Stevens and Margaret Dorrell Rogerson published the entire gathering in Modern Philology, fully annotated with a translation. Rather than repeat that edition, we have chosen here to represent the MS more properly in its present state of preservation. Besides the flood damage, ff 253-4 have split (or been deliberately slashed) from the bottom halfway up the folios. The breaks have been patched with paper, now discolouring and obliterating the writing underneath. Other holes in the parchment are similarly patched. We have printed what is now visible and given the readings of earlier editors in footnotes. Unless otherwise indicated, the readings in the first list are from Smith, in the second from Davies. We have not attempted to reproduce the complex variety of braces and lines connecting craft names and pageant descriptions. Although the second list (which is undated) has traditionally been assumed to be later than the first, we have preserved the gathering intact. The hands are similar, though not identical. The second list (on f 255) is in two columns.

York Prototype added note, Sergi 2025:
Beadle and Meredith append a facsimile of this record to their facsimile of the York Register (The York Play: A Facsimile of British Library MS Additional 35290, University of Leeds, 1983); therein, they describe the damage to this document in detail: "The leaves containing the Ordo paginarum and its companion pieces have suffered considerably over the course of time. Folios 253 and 254 are affected by a cut from about two-thirds of the way up f.253 and half way up f.254, which extends to the foot of both leaves. A portion of the leaf is also missing at the foot of f.253. These cuts have been repaired first by the attaching of parchment patches on the corners of the versos of both leaves, and secondly by gluing over the cut areas paper which has turned an opaque yellowy-brown. The bottom edge of f.255 has also been repaired with a parchment patch, and damage to the leaf above that has been covered with more of the same paper. This paper has also been used for a patch towards the foot of f.252v. Considerable portions of the text are thus affected by these repairs, especially on f.253v where nearly half of the page is covered by the paper patch. Furthermore the manuscript suffered in the 1892 flood when the City archives were still at the Guildhall. The level reached by the flood-water can be seen clearly on each leaf. The area above the mark left by the water is still clean and easily read, that below is stained and the writing has in many cases been washed away. This has particularly affected the original entries, and in many cases the additions and alterations alone have survived. The gradually darkening paper patches present the greatest problem, to the extent that in many cases it is no longer possible to read what is written beneath. Ultra-violet light unfortunately makes the writing underneath the paper patches even less easy to read. Most of the writing which has been washed off, however, is still legible either in bright sun or with ultra-violet light. The present state of the leaves clearly gives greater importance to the earlier transcriptions of this section of the manuscript, especially those prior to the flood of 1892, and prior to the paper repairs that followed soon after that date."

The "earlier transcriptions" thus drawn on by REED 1979 (as well as Stevens/Dorrell 1974, see above), to supply the text where floods and repairs have made the original unreadable, are the Ordo and the proclamation, are in Lucy Toulmin Smith's Introduction to her 1885 York Plays edition (Oxford: Clarendon Press) at pp. xix-xxvii and xxxiv, and in the Appendix to Francis Drake's 1736 Eboracum: Or the History and Antiquities of the City of York (London: William Bowyer) at Vol. 1, pp. xxx-xxxii. Neither of those transcriptions includes the second pageant list after the proclamation; for that, REED 1979 consults Robert Davies's 1843 Extracts from the Municipal Records of the City of York (London: J.B. Nichols and Son), at pp. 233-5. Conveniently, as of 2025 at least, all three of these early editions are available online: the Smith 1885 edition can be downloaded free of charge at Google Books, the Drake 1736 is at Eighteenth Century Collections Online, and the Davies 1843 is part of Gale's Making of Modern Law: Legal Treatises, 1800-1926 series.

Drake 1736 only provides the Latin portions of the Ordo in a then-modern English translation, which Smith 1885 rightly calls "very incorrect," but as the earliest extant witness to the damaged portions of the Ordo and proclamation it cannot be dismissed entirely. Given Smith's concern with correcting past mistranslation, and given that Stevens/Dorrell 1974 check her work fully against the extant original and find only very few corrections (at p. 58), I am inclined, as REED 1979 and Stevens/Dorrell 1974 do, to take Smith's transcription as the primary and very reliable authority on the Ordo and proclamation. Note that Smith inserts editorial punctuation for clarity, which I, following the example of Stevens and Dorrell, leave out of my transcriptions of her work here.

The full English translation included in this record, reproduced exactly from REED 1979 except where noted, incorporates those interpolated readings invisibly, smoothing out the version of the text that I suspect most readers will consult directly. But the record digitized here is anything but smooth: it is dated 1415, but is very much a palimpsest, with additions and changes made across the subsequent centuries of York Corpus Christi play production. Readers should pay close attention to the marks and notes; note too that my eREED EATS tags categorize all of the occupational collectives mentioned herein under "guilds," though some were not incorporated or recognized as such.
